glucose chains — test bench

One linkage, measured off the real disaccharide, applied over and over. Nothing here draws a helix or a ribbon: a rigid repeat is always a rotation about an axis plus a slide along it, so whatever shape appears is what that glycosidic bond does when you do it twelve times.

a chain this long is mostly hydrogen; hiding the nonpolar C–H leaves the backbone and the hydroxyls, which is what the shape is about

Cellulose — β-1,4. The bridge leaves C1 equatorial, in the ring's plane, so each residue hands off to the next straight ahead. Half a turn per residue is a flat two-fold ribbon, which is what stacks into fibres nothing human can digest.

Galactan — β-1,4, one OH flipped. Galactose is glucose with C4's oxygen on the other side of the ring — and in a 1→4 chain that oxygen is the bridge. So this varies the acceptor end while starch varies the donor end. Same β linkage as cellulose, same torsions, one substituent moved. Prediction, not a measured polymer — see the note under the readout.

Lactose — the control, and not a chain. Same β-1,4 bond as galactan, same galactose donating C1, same axial C4 on that ring. The acceptor is glucose instead of galactose, and that is the only difference. You digest this one. It cannot chain: the glucose's C4 is spent on the bridge and its C1 is the free reducing end, so this is the whole molecule.

Starch — α-1,4. The bridge leaves C1 axial, out of the plane, so each residue hands off at an angle and the chain curls into a helix loose enough for amylase to reach into. Twelve residues cover 17 Å here, against cellulose's 59.
