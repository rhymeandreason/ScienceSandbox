Test — solvent-excluded surface over the ribbon.
The tetramer as folding/ribbon.js draws it, with the molecular surface laid over the top as a translucent skin. The surface is baked, not solved here: tools/ses.js builds it offline from 2HHB's 4 556 heavy atoms with a 1.4 Å water probe, and the page just loads a mesh. The same job took 3Dmol 5.7 s and Mol* 9.4 s in viewer-compare.html.

s toggles the surface, c cycles what it is coloured by, v swaps normal haemoglobin (2HHB) for sickle (2HBS). Chain colour says which subunit a patch belongs to; hydrophobicity is the Kyte–Doolittle scale, and it is the one that carries a lesson — the greasy patches are where another molecule could stick.

The swap is the experiment. Both molecules are drawn at the same 0.7 Å grid, and 2HBS is rotated onto 2HHB by a superposition stored as data, not baked into either — so nothing you see when you press v is the frame moving. Watch the silhouette, then switch to β6 and watch again.

The two fit to …, so the silhouette is the wrong place to look — and the area difference in the panel is two different crystals' surface side chains, not the mutation, which is four atoms. Sickle-cell is not a misshapen protein. It is one charged residue swapped for a greasy one, on the outside.

The β6 patch is its own mesh, so it carries its own colour and opacity — it can stay solid over a skin at 0.28, or over no skin at all. Left on follow chemistry its colour is the finding: charged blue becomes greasy orange. Pin it to one colour instead and you are asking the other question — whether the patch moves.
