Myoglobin

One chain wrapped around one iron atom. It holds oxygen in your muscle until the muscle needs it. This was the first protein to have its shape solved.

— — at the PDB →

what it is

A single chain, all helix, folded around a hole

PDB Molecule of the Month article →

Myoglobin's fold creates a gap in the middle that holds a heme group. Heme is a flat ring molecule that holds an iron ion in its center. That iron ion binds oxygen.

Myoglobin is why muscle is red, and why some meat is darker than others: more myoglobin, more colour.

the site

Oxygen, held at an angle

Here is the same protein with oxygen bound. The O₂ sits on top of the iron, leaning over rather than standing straight up, and a histidine side chain above it leans in to steady it with a hydrogen bond. Below the ring, a second histidine anchors the iron from the other side.

Look at what the iron does. In the empty protein it is pulled slightly below the plane of the ring, towards the histidine holding it. Bind oxygen and it snaps into the plane. It is a small move for a molecule, and in myoglobin’s four-chain cousin haemoglobin that same move is the whole mechanism.

carbon monoxide

The same site, the wrong molecule

Carbon monoxide binds to the same iron, in the same place, and it binds hard. Once CO is on, the site is occupied and the protein is useless for the job it exists to do. That is carbon monoxide poisoning: not damage to the tissue, but a parking space taken.

The protein does defend itself. That distal histidine, the one steadying the oxygen from above, is shaped to favour a bent molecule — and CO would rather stand straight. It cannot make the site prefer oxygen outright, but it narrows the gap by a large factor, which is why a lungful of ordinary air does not poison you.

The Breakthrough

The first protein structure solved, and it came out of a whale

John Kendrew determined the atomic three-dimensional structure of myoglobin using X-ray crystallography in 1957 and 1958. Kendrew chose myoglobin because it is small (about 1/4 the size of hemoglobin), stable, and crystallizes easily. Yet he still had to examine 110 crystals and measure the intensities of around 250,000 X-ray reflections to solve its structure. Kendrew shared the 1962 Nobel Prize with Max Perutz, who was doing the same work for haemoglobin.

nobelprize.org →

Kendrew used sperm whale cells because he needed a lot of pure protein and diving mammals are packed with it. Whale muscle is nearly black with myoglobin, which is part of how they can dive for so long. The fold, the ring, the iron and the two histidines are the same in humans.
