ATP synthase

A rotor spun by protons falling across a membrane, wringing ATP out of ADP and phosphate. Seven depositions, one camera, and not one of them moving.

what the turning is for

Two subunits of one crystal, either side of letting go. Superposed on the P-loop, the eight residues that grip the phosphates, so the site stays put and the domain swing is what moves. Releasing that ADP is what the rotation does, and it is where this enzyme's energy actually goes.

the machine

Three stills, not three frames. These are superposed on the stator, so the head and the rotor are where they are relative to the part that holds still. The order they are listed in is an argument from kinetics rather than something anybody watched.

You cannot see a proton. It is one positive charge with no electrons, and a structure is a map of where the electrons are. So the ion in the rotor ring is a sodium: 2WGM is the Na⁺-driven version of this enzyme, chosen by the depositors precisely because its ion scatters. Everything about the binding site — one ion per subunit, buried in the middle of the membrane, coordinated by a glutamate — is the same story, and the ion is a stand-in.

Eleven here, eight in the human enzyme. The number of c-subunits is not a constant, so neither is the number of protons a full turn costs, so neither is the protons-per-ATP figure a textbook quotes. Three ATP per turn, and eight or eleven or fourteen protons depending on the organism. Compare the assembly row on this view against the human ones.

The chain labels travel with the rotor. The depositors name the three β subunits by which conformation each is in, so as γ turns the labels follow it round: state 1's chain D is state 3's chain E. Comparing by letter therefore measures the relabelling and not the motion, and reported 60 Å for a head that did not move. Each chain is matched to the best-fitting copy of the same subunit instead, and the what moved row and the pairing list below it are what came back.

The two sites are fitted on eight residues, not on the chain. β-E is open because its C-terminal domain has swung away from the nucleotide. Fitting whole subunits would split that difference and misalign both sites in order to make the domains agree; fitting the P-loop puts the chemistry in one place and leaves the swing visible. The what moved row is the residual over the rest of the chain, and it is the swing.

There is no ATP on this bench. The only deposition that shows one in this enzyme's catalytic site holds AMP-PNP — ATP with the oxygen between the last two phosphates replaced by nitrogen, so it cannot be broken and the site can be photographed clamped shut. That view was cut at review, because a ligand chosen by the experimenter is a weaker thing to build a lesson on than two sites that simply are what they are. So the product is here and the substrate is not, and that gap is a fact about what has been crystallised rather than about the enzyme.

Almost all the ATP in you is made by one machine: a rotor turned by protons falling across a membrane, mechanically wringing ATP out of ADP and phosphate. It is the only enzyme most people meet that is literally a motor, and it runs at over a hundred revolutions a second.

Two pieces, two jobs. F₀ sits in the membrane: a ring of c-subunits, eight to fifteen depending on the species, each carrying one proton across in single file. The flow of protons down their gradient turns the ring, the way water turns a mill wheel. F₁ sticks out into the matrix: three α and three β subunits alternating around a central shaft, the γ subunit, which is bolted to the ring. As γ turns inside the head it deforms each β subunit in turn.

That deformation is the chemistry. Boyer's binding change mechanism says the three catalytic sites are never in the same state at once: one open, one loosely holding ADP and phosphate, one clamped shut. A third of a turn moves each site on to the next state. The surprise is that making the ATP bond costs almost nothing — the energy goes into releasing it. The finished ATP is stuck to the tight site, and the rotation is what pries it off. The two site views on this bench are the two ends of that release: holding, and empty.

Walker solved the F₁ structure in 1994 and found what Boyer had predicted from kinetics alone: three β subunits caught in three different states in one crystal. They shared the Nobel in 1997. The gradient that drives the whole thing is what the electron transport chain exists to build, which makes this the enzyme that chain has been working towards.

It also runs backwards, and does. Spend ATP instead of making it and the motor becomes a pump, pushing protons out. A cell starved of oxygen would burn its remaining ATP that way in minutes, which is what the inhibitor protein IF1 is for: it jams the rotor in one direction only. It is drawn in violet on the human views, and it is the one chain there that is not part of the enzyme.

Turn the molecule, then copy. The assemblies already open on the rotation axis, which is the one thing about this enzyme's frame that is never in doubt, so this is for choosing the roll — and for the four single-subunit views, which have no axis and open in the deposited frame until somebody picks one.
