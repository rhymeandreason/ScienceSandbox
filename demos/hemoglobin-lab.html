The molecule

One β chain of human haemoglobin — 146 amino acids, measured by X-ray crystallography at 1.74 Å as PDB 2HHB. Your blood carries about 270 million copies of the finished protein per red cell.

Level 1 · primary

The order of the 146 amino acids, and nothing else — the only part your DNA specifies. Everything after it follows.

The page opens on the whole chain as real atoms — drag left and right to read along all 146. A link in the chain is N — C — C, repeating, with each residue's C joined to the next one's N. That join is the peptide bond.

Not drawn: the side chains — the part that differs from one amino acid to the next. They are left out because this animation moves only the backbone.

Level 2 · secondary

103 backbone hydrogen bonds form. It turns eight stretches of the chain into eight α-helices. There are no β-sheet anywhere in this protein.

Level 3 · tertiary

This actually happens at the same time as secondary structures forming, but we have made it more separate in this animation to differentiate. Watch the two numbers at the right. Around the two-thirds mark the hydrogen-bond count stops climbing while the molecule keeps shrinking — from 204 Å across to 45. The helices are already made; now they pack against each other, driven by water pushing the oily side chains inward. The pocket that forms in the middle is where the iron and its oxygen go.

Level 4 · quaternary

The working molecule is four finished chains — two α and two β — each gripping an iron. In this animation, watch them arrive one at a time: α1 first, because α1 with your β makes the pair that binds tightest and comes together first, then the second pair. Nothing about the chains changes as they dock; level 4 is which finished chains touch which, and that is what lets them cooperate — oxygen binding to one iron makes the next one easier.

The dashes that appear as each chain lands are hydrogen bonds — the same bond act 2 used to build the helices, now holding two chains together. There are only eight of them between α1 and your β chain. Most of an interface is not bonds: it is hydrophobic packing, the same thing that drove level 3.

A tetramer is four finished tertiary structures.

What this animation fakes

Real folding is thermal chaos, not a smooth collapse. More than that: this is an animation of a known answer, not a prediction — and it is made backwards. The computer takes the real measured structure and pulls it apart into the open chain, then plays that film in reverse. So every frame you see is a step on a path that ends, exactly, at the real crystal structure. The order of events is right; the path is invented.

Where the ribbon comes from

The eight helices are 2HHB's own HELIX records — what the crystallographers assigned, not a guess by us. Running the standard algorithm (DSSP) on the same coordinates agrees on 83% of residues and finds no β-sheet either.

Why the helices move as units

Once a helix is made, this animation treats it as a rigid object on a string — it can swing and slide, but not bend or unravel. That is a real model of folding, not a shortcut: helices form first and then drift together and dock. Real helices do flex a little, especially at their ends, so this is an approximation — a good one over the few microseconds that matter here.
