The molecule

One β chain of human haemoglobin — 146 amino acids, measured by X-ray crystallography at 1.74 Å as PDB 2HHB. Your blood carries about 270 million copies of the finished protein per red cell.

Level 1 · primary

The order of the 146 amino acids, and nothing else — the only part your DNA specifies. The stretched-out chain you see before you press play is level 1. Everything after it follows.

Level 2 · secondary

103 backbone hydrogen bonds form. 78 of them link a residue to the one four places along the chain, and that spacing is what makes a spiral instead of a loop — it turns eight stretches of the chain into eight α-helices. Almost all the rest are i→i+3, at the ends of those same helices. There is no β-sheet anywhere in this protein.

Level 3 · tertiary

Watch the two numbers at the right. Around the two-thirds mark the hydrogen-bond count stops climbing while the molecule keeps shrinking — from 204 Å across to 45. The helices are already made; now they pack against each other, driven by water pushing the oily side chains inward. The pocket that forms in the middle is where the iron and its oxygen go.

Level 4 · quaternary

The working molecule is four finished chains — two α (141 amino acids each) and two β (146) — each gripping an iron. After the fold, watch them arrive one at a time: α1 first, because α1 with your β makes the pair that binds tightest and comes together first, then the second pair. Nothing about the chains changes as they dock; level 4 is which finished chains touch which, and that is what lets them cooperate — oxygen binding to one iron makes the next one easier.

The dashes that appear as each chain lands are hydrogen bonds across the new interface — the same bond act 2 used to build the helices, now holding two chains together. There are only eight of them between α1 and your β chain, across about thirty residues in contact. Most of an interface is not bonds: it is hydrophobic packing, the same thing that drove level 3, and it is left undrawn here for the same reason it was left undrawn there — it has no endpoints.

Why this order: measuring contact on the crystal structure (α carbons within 8.5 Å), α1 makes 72 contacts with your β chain and the second α makes 43 — and the two β chains never touch at all. The big tight pair forms first; the weaker join is the one that shifts when oxygen binds.

Those three chains are not folded by this page. They are the deposited crystal coordinates, moved into place — placed, not solved. A tetramer is four finished tertiary structures, so that is the honest way to show it.

What this animation fakes

Real folding is thermal chaos, not a smooth collapse. More than that: this is an animation of a known answer, not a prediction — and it is made backwards. The computer takes the real measured structure and pulls it apart into the open chain, then plays that film in reverse. So every frame you see is a step on a path that ends, exactly, at the real crystal structure. The order of events is right; the path is invented.

Where the ribbon comes from

The eight helices are 2HHB's own HELIX records — what the crystallographers assigned, not a guess by us. Running the standard algorithm (DSSP) on the same coordinates agrees on 83% of residues and finds no β-sheet either.

Why the helices move as units

Once a helix is made, this animation treats it as a rigid object on a string — it can swing and slide, but not bend or unravel. That is a real model of folding, not a shortcut: helices form first and then drift together and dock, which is why the bond count can sit still while the shape keeps changing. Real helices do flex a little, especially at their ends, so this is an approximation — a good one over the few microseconds that matter here.
