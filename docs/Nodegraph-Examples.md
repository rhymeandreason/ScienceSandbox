## Exceptional Examples

Kleptoplasty is a good instinct, and it's worth being clear about *why* it's good, because that determines which other examples earn a place.

*Elysia chlorotica* isn't just charming. It's an animal that eats algae, keeps the chloroplasts alive in its gut lining, and photosynthesizes for months. That single fact stresses four things you've built: the producer/consumer boundary in ecology, endosymbiosis as an ongoing process rather than an ancient event, the chloroplast's dependence on nuclear genes it no longer has, and the autotroph/heterotroph distinction turning out to be leaky.

## The test for a good exemplar

Charm alone produces a trivia pile. The ones worth adding **break a rule the reader just learned**, and the breakage teaches something. `Elysia` passes hard: it violates "animals don't photosynthesize" and the violation explains what endosymbiosis actually requires.

Model these as their own edge type — `challenges` or `edge-case-of` — pointing at the generalization they strain. Rank 2, so they don't clutter the spine, but findable from the node they complicate.

## Others that pass

**Endosymbiosis / cell** *Paramecium bursaria* keeping live *Chlorella* — a symbiosis you can watch, and the alga can still leave. Endosymbiosis caught in the act, at a stage between free-living and organelle.

**Water / cohesion** Tardigrades in anhydrobiosis, replacing their water with trehalose glass. Makes the point that water isn't incidental — remove it and you must substitute something with similar hydrogen-bonding behaviour.

**Proteins** Antifreeze proteins in Antarctic icefish, binding ice crystals to stop growth. Structure-function with an unusual target. Icefish also have no hemoglobin at all, which challenges the "oxygen transport requires hemoglobin" story from physiology.

**Respiration** *Loricifera* in the Mediterranean anoxic basin — animals with no mitochondria, living permanently without oxygen. Challenges "animals need oxygen" directly.

**Photosynthesis** Deep-sea green sulfur bacteria photosynthesizing on hydrothermal vent glow. Redefines what counts as a light source.

**Molecular genetics** *Oxytricha* rearranging its genome from thousands of fragments every generation. Challenges "the genome is a stable ordered sequence."

**Mendelian** Haplodiploidy in bees — males have no father and no sons. Breaks diploid assumptions cleanly.

**Evolution** Antibiotic resistance genes recovered from permafrost predating antibiotics — resistance existed before the selection pressure. That's the Lamarckian misconception killed with evidence rather than argument.

**Viruses** Mimivirus, larger than some bacteria, with genes for protein synthesis machinery. Sits right on the is-it-alive boundary you built. Virophages infecting it complicate matters further.

**Scale ladder** *Caulerpa* — a single cell metres long. Breaks the SA:V argument for cell size, and the exception is instructive because it has workarounds.

**Homeostasis** Wood frogs freezing solid, heart stopped, and thawing in spring.

## Two cautions

**Density.** Roughly one or two per unit. More and the exceptions crowd out the rules, and a reader who meets five weird organisms before internalizing the pattern learns neither.

**Sequencing.** These only work as `challenges` if the generalization is already held. Rank them so they surface *after* the node they complicate, not alongside it. A reader who meets *Elysia* before understanding autotrophy just learns that biology is arbitrary.

## Why it's worth doing

These are also where the graph shape pays off. A textbook can't afford a page for *Elysia*, so it becomes a sidebar or gets cut. In your structure it's a node with two edges, costs almost nothing, and is discoverable from exactly the concept it complicates.

That's a real advantage worth using deliberately rather than as decoration — the marginal cost of an interesting exception is far lower here than in linear media.
