# What students will ask about water

Anticipated student questions for `demos/water-lab.html`, grouped so rephrasings
of the same question sit together, with the answer to give and whether the page
already handles it.

**Coverage**: ✓ on the page · ~ partly · ✗ not covered.

## Step 1 — A Polar Molecule

| What they ask | Answer to give | |
| --- | --- | :-: |
| *Why is it bent?*<br>*Why doesn't it go straight across like CO₂?*<br>*Why 104.5° and not 109.5°?* | Oxygen holds two **lone pairs** as well as the two bonds. Lone pairs spread out more than bonding pairs, so they squeeze the hydrogens down from the 109.5° a four-way split would give. | ✗ |
| *Where's the negative part? I only see a red ball.*<br>*What's on the other side of the oxygen?* | The two lone pairs. They sit on the far side of the oxygen, they are the actual δ− end, and later they're what accept hydrogen bonds. Nothing on screen shows them, so this question has no visual answer right now. | ✗ |
| *So is water charged or not?*<br>*You said neutral but also negative?*<br>*Is δ− a real charge?* | The molecule is neutral overall. δ means a **partial** charge, a fraction of an electron's worth, from electrons sitting closer to one end. No electron has been given away, which is what separates this from an ion. | ✗ |
| *Why is oxygen greedier than hydrogen?*<br>*What is electronegativity?* | Electronegativity is how hard a nucleus pulls on shared electrons. Oxygen has 8 protons to hydrogen's 1, so the shared pair drifts toward oxygen. | ~ |
| *If it were straight, would it still be polar?* | No. CO₂ has two polar bonds pointing opposite ways, so they cancel. Shape decides polarity as much as the bonds do, which is exactly why the bend matters. | ✗ |
| *Is polar covalent basically ionic?*<br>*Where's the cutoff?* | Same spectrum, different degree. Shared unevenly is polar covalent; pulled off completely is ionic. No sharp line, but a big electronegativity difference (Na and Cl) means transfer. | ✗ |
| *Why is the oxygen red?*<br>*Is that its real color?* | The standard chemistry color code, so you can read any model at a glance. Molecules are far smaller than light's wavelength, so they have no color at all. | ✗ |

## Step 2 — Hydrogen Bonds

| What they ask | Answer to give | |
| --- | --- | :-: |
| *Is a hydrogen bond a real bond?*<br>*Or are they just kind of stuck together?*<br>*Is anything shared?* | Nothing is shared. It's an attraction between a δ+ hydrogen and a δ− lone pair on a neighbor, roughly a twentieth the strength of the covalent bond inside the molecule. | ✓ |
| *How many can one water grab?*<br>*Why four?* | Four: it donates through its two hydrogens and accepts through its two lone pairs. Water is unusual in having as many donors as acceptors, which is what lets it build a full network instead of small clusters. Unanswerable without the lone pairs from step 1. | ✗ |
| *Do they stay stuck?*<br>*It looks like a net.*<br>*Is that the same bond the whole time?* | No. Each lasts a picosecond or so, then breaks and re-forms with someone else. The network is real but no individual link is, which is why water flows instead of being a solid. The static-looking render is the most misread thing in the lesson. | ✗ |
| *Does anything else do this?*<br>*Or is it a water thing?* | Any H attached to N, O, or F. It holds the two strands of DNA together and gives proteins their helices and sheets, so this one idea comes back all semester. | ✗ |
| *A twentieth of what?*<br>*Why doesn't the whole molecule fall apart then?* | About 20 kJ/mol against roughly 460 for the O–H covalent bond. Ordinary heat easily breaks the weak one and never touches the strong one, so molecules rearrange without coming apart. | ~ |
| *Are they actually touching?*<br>*Why's there a gap in the picture?* | Atoms have no hard edge. The gap is roughly where the electron clouds start pushing back, a real distance, about twice the length of the covalent bond. | ✗ |
| *Is this why water sticks to itself?*<br>*Why bugs walk on it?*<br>*Why it beads on a car?* | Yes. Surface molecules have neighbors only below and beside them, so the surface pulls itself tight. Same H-bonds, different consequence. Cohesion has no home in the lesson and they go there unprompted. | ✗ |
| *Why is water a liquid at room temperature?*<br>*Shouldn't something this small be a gas?* | H₂S is heavier than water and boils at −60°C. Water boils at +100°C purely because H-bonds hold it together. That 160-degree gap is the whole payoff of this step. | ✗ |

## Step 3 — Specific Heat

| What they ask | Answer to give | |
| --- | --- | :-: |
| *Is heat the same as temperature?*<br>*Isn't more heat just a higher number?* | No. Temperature is the average speed of the molecules; heat is total energy poured in. Water absorbs a lot of the second while barely moving the first, and that gap *is* specific heat. The slider reads °C while the lesson is about joules — the misconception that quietly wrecks the step. | ✗ |
| *If energy goes into breaking bonds, where does it go?*<br>*Does it just disappear?* | Into stored potential energy of separated molecules, like lifting a book. Let them re-form and you get it straight back as heat. | ✗ |
| *You said it resists heating, but they're obviously speeding up?* | They do speed up, just far less per joule than most liquids, because much of each joule goes to bond-breaking. Compare a metal pan heating in seconds on the same burner. | ~ |
| *How does sweat cool me if it leaves at my temperature?*<br>*Why does getting out of a pool feel cold?* | Only the fastest molecules escape, so what stays behind has a lower average speed. Evaporation skims off the hottest and the temperature drops. | ~ |
| *Is boiling the same as evaporating?* | Evaporation is only the surface and happens at any temperature. Boiling is when even the interior can form vapor, so bubbles appear throughout. The sim shows both. | ✗ |
| *Why is the pool freezing but the concrete burns my feet?*<br>*Why is the coast milder than inland?* | Same sunlight, wildly different specific heat. Concrete's is about a fifth of water's, so it races up while the pool barely moves. Scale that up and you get ocean climate. | ~ |
| *Why 100? Who picked that?* | Backwards: the Celsius scale was *defined* by water, 0 at freezing and 100 at boiling, at sea-level pressure. Up a mountain water boils cooler. | ✗ |

## Step 4 — Why Ice Floats

| What they ask | Answer to give | |
| --- | --- | :-: |
| *How is it less dense if it's the same molecules?*<br>*Do they shrink?* | Nothing shrinks; the **spacing** grows. Locked at their preferred H-bond angles, molecules hold each other further apart than jostling liquid does, so the same count fills more volume. | ✓ |
| *Why hexagons?*<br>*Why not squares?* | Each water points its four H-bonds at the corners of a tetrahedron. Tile that fixed angle through space and hexagonal rings are what you get, which is also why snowflakes have six arms. | ✗ |
| *Do they stop moving in ice?*<br>*Is 0°C where motion stops?* | They vibrate in place, they just don't travel. All motion stopping is absolute zero, −273°C. | ✗ |
| *Isn't water heaviest at 4°C, not 0?* | Yes, 3.98°C. Cooling packs molecules tighter until the lattice starts forming, then the open structure wins and it expands again. `tempParams()` keeps water liquid down to freezing but does not model the density peak. | ✗ |
| *So lakes only freeze on top?*<br>*What about the fish?* | The ice floats and insulates what's below, so deep water stays liquid all winter. If ice sank, lakes would freeze solid from the bottom and stay that way. | ✓ |
| *Does anything else float on its own melted version?* | Almost nothing. Silicon and bismuth do; no liquid you'd meet in a kitchen does. | ~ |
| *Is this why my soda explodes in the freezer?*<br>*Why pipes burst?* | Same fact. Expanding on freezing means the ice has to go somewhere, and it wins against aluminum and copper. | ✗ |

## Step 5 — The Universal Solvent

| What they ask | Answer to give | |
| --- | --- | :-: |
| *If it dissolves everything, why not oil?*<br>*Why not plastic?*<br>*Why does salad dressing separate?* | "Universal" is an overstatement. Water dissolves what it can hydrogen-bond to or attract; oil is nonpolar and offers nothing to grip, so water sticks to itself and squeezes the oil out. The phrase invites this every time — one sentence closes it and points at `demos/solvation-lab.html`. | ✗ |
| *Did the sodium turn back into metal?*<br>*Did it take its electron back?*<br>*Is the salt broken?* | No electrons move here. Na⁺ stays Na⁺ and Cl⁻ stays Cl⁻; water only pulls the two apart and surrounds each. Dissolving is separating, not reacting. This page is where they first see ions split, so the claim belongs here; `demos/solvation-lab.html` already makes it. | ✗ |
| *Which end points at which ion again?*<br>*Why do some waters flip around?* | Opposites face inward. Na⁺ is positive, so oxygens (δ−) aim at it; Cl⁻ is negative, so hydrogens (δ+) aim at it. The single most-repeated question in the lesson. | ✓ |
| *Why six?*<br>*Is it always six?* | Six is how many waters fit around an ion that size. Bigger ions hold more, smaller ones fewer, and the shell constantly swaps members rather than being a fixed cage. | ~ |
| *Does the water get colder when salt dissolves?*<br>*Does dissolving take energy or give it off?* | Slightly colder for table salt: pulling the ions apart costs a bit more than hydrating them gives back. They can feel it in a glass at home. | ✗ |
| *Can I keep adding salt forever?*<br>*What happens if I max the slider?* | No. Around 360 g per liter it's saturated and the rest just sits there. That's also why road salt stops working near −21°C, the coldest brine that stays liquid — `saltShift()` caps ΔTf at exactly that eutectic. | ~ |
| *Why does salt lower freezing but raise boiling?*<br>*Is melting point the same as freezing point?* | Dissolved particles get in the way of molecules leaving the liquid, in either direction: harder to lock into ice, harder to escape as vapor. Melting and freezing point are the same temperature, approached from opposite sides. | ~ |
| *But people say salting the pot makes it boil faster?* | It doesn't; it raises the boiling point slightly. A cook's pinch shifts it by a fraction of a degree, which is a flavour decision, not a physics one. | ✗ |
| *Where did the salt go?*<br>*Can I get it back?* | Still there, spread out as separated ions. Boil the water off and the crystals come back unchanged, which is the proof that nothing reacted. | ✗ |
| *Is this why you can't drink seawater?*<br>*Why does salt make you thirsty?* | Water follows dissolved particles across membranes, so seawater pulls water out of your cells faster than it supplies it. That's osmosis, and it's the membrane lesson. | ✗ |

## Questions about the simulation itself

| What they ask | Answer to give | |
| --- | --- | :-: |
| *Is this real or did you make it look cool?*<br>*Do they really move that fast?* | The geometry, the angle, the shell counts and the freezing shift are real numbers. The speed is not: actual molecules reshuffle about a trillion times faster than this, far too fast to watch. One honest line covers it, and students trust the rest more once it's said. | ✗ |
| *How many are there really?*<br>*Is a drop just a few dozen?* | A single drop holds about 10²¹ molecules. The screen shows a few dozen so you can see any of them at all. | ✗ |
| *Why are some bonds sticks and some dotted?* | Solid stick means a covalent bond, shared electrons, permanent. Dotted means a hydrogen bond, attraction only, temporary. | ~ |
| *Why start with water? Isn't this biology?* | Every reaction in you happens in water, and shape decides what a protein does. The two ideas from this lesson, polarity and H-bonds, are what fold proteins and pair DNA. | ✗ |

## The five gaps worth building in

1. **Lone pairs in step 1.** Load-bearing: they explain the bend, the count of
   four in step 2, and which end faces which ion in step 5. `demos/lobes/lobes.js`
   already draws them.
2. **"They break and re-form constantly."** One line in step 2, against a render
   that reads as a fixed net.
3. **Heat is not temperature.** Step 3 is about joules while its only control is
   labelled °C.
4. **A counterexample for "universal solvent."** Oil, one sentence, pointing at
   `demos/solvation-lab.html`.
5. **Cohesion and surface tension.** No home in the lesson, and students arrive
   there on their own from step 2.
