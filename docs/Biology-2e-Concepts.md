# Loertscher Threshold Concepts × OpenStax Biology 2e × AP Bio CED

Loertscher and colleagues set out to identify which concepts in biochemistry function as *threshold* concepts — ideas that transform a student's understanding of the discipline and without which they cannot progress. Rather than assert a list, they ran a consensus-building process: more than 75 faculty members and 50 undergraduate students participated through faculty workshops and student interviews, modeled on threshold-concept work in other disciplines, and an iterative process narrowed the results to five prioritized concepts — steady state, biochemical pathway dynamics and regulation, the physical basis of interactions, thermodynamics of macromolecular structure formation, and free energy. The paper is a characterization study, not an intervention: it establishes what to target, explicitly framing the list as a working subset rather than an exhaustive account, with the goal of directing future instructional and assessment development.

Loertscher, J., Green, D., Lewis, J. E., Lin, S., & Minderhout, V. (2014). Identification of threshold concepts for biochemistry. *CBE—Life Sciences Education*, 13(3), 516–528. https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.14-04-0066

**Reading the failure modes**

They sort into two groups by what they cost to fix:

*Cheap* — displacement and sequencing inversion are ordering problems. The content exists; it's in the wrong place relative to what needs it. A teacher can fix both without adding material.

*Expensive* — dispersion, depth mismatch, and absence all require adding something: a consolidation lesson that doesn't exist in either framework, quantitative content AP doesn't require, or scope the course has declined.

The uncomfortable pattern is that **glycolysis sits at the intersection of the two expensive ones**. It depends on free energy (depth mismatch) and demonstrates pathway dynamics (displacement, cheap) — but the displacement fix only helps if the free-energy foundation is there, and under AP it isn't. Resequencing regulation earlier gives students a dynamics story they lack the energetics to interpret.

**Caveat on the diagnosis column**

It's strongest for free energy, where I can point to specific documented misconceptions and where the ATP-bond literature is solid. It's weakest for physical basis of interactions and macromolecular thermodynamics, where I'm reasoning from coverage depth rather than from evidence about what students actually get wrong. Treat those two rows as hypotheses.
