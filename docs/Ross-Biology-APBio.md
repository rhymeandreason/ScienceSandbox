## Reading the three-way

**AP wins three rows** — probability (chi-square, Hardy-Weinberg), SA:V (named topic), hypothesis testing (Science Practices throughout). All three are cases where the CED's framework-level design does something a textbook's linear structure can't.

**The book wins two** — energy transformation (§§27–30 block, plus ecosystem-level §249) and SA:V *application*(§§173, 206), though both are partly out of AP scope.

**Both fail identically on three** — dynamic equilibrium, variation, subcellular↔macroscopic. These are the rows where two independently designed frameworks make the same error, which is the strongest evidence you have that the problem is structural rather than editorial.

**And one row is the finding.** Subcellular↔macroscopic has no home section and no CED topic. Two frameworks, one majors-level and one high-school, neither of which assigns responsibility for the cross-level move that every chapter after cell structure silently requires. That's the general form of the glycolysis question you opened with.

A specific diagnostic claim: glycolysis is hard not because of the ten steps, but because understanding it requires crossing thresholds the curriculum doesn't hand you. Specifically —

* It's a **flux system taught as an arrow diagram**. The representation encodes direct causation; the phenomenon is emergent (Chi). Students memorize a sequence and can't reason about regulation.

* It depends on **free energy**, which arrives qualitatively — exergonic/endergonic as labels, no ΔG.

* It demonstrates **pathway dynamics**, but regulation is displaced — §39 in *Biology 2e*, Unit 4 in the CED.

* And it requires the **subcellular↔macroscopic link** that no section in either framework owns.

So "the glycolysis problem" came to mean: *a topic whose difficulty is structural — located in the sequencing and ownership of prerequisite concepts, not in the topic's own content.* Glycolysis is where four separate failure modes intersect, which is why it makes a good diagnostic case.
